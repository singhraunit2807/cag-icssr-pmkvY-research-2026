# Digital Governance and the Reliability of Skill-Development Administration: Evidence from the CAG Performance Audit of PMKVY

## Abstract

Large-scale skill-development programmes increasingly depend on digital systems for beneficiary registration, training management, assessment, certification, monitoring and post-certification processes. The presence of such systems, however, does not by itself establish that the information generated through them is complete, verifiable, traceable or useful for public oversight. This article examines this issue through the Performance Audit of skill development under the Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY), presented as CAG Report No. 20 of 2025. The study uses qualitative document analysis, descriptive quantitative analysis and comparative analysis of subsequent policy and digital-system developments. The main audit evidence covers the first three phases of PMKVY during 2015–22 and eight selected States. The analysis organises audit findings around data reliability, beneficiary verification, evidence retention, monitoring and system validation, Centre-State information access, feedback and outcome tracking. It then examines how PMKVY 4.0 and the Skill India Digital Hub (SIDH) respond to several of these control issues. The findings indicate that digital infrastructure is only one part of governance reliability: the usefulness of a digital programme depends on the quality of data, strength of verification, retention of evidence, validity of monitoring signals, accessibility of information and connection between feedback and course correction. The study therefore proposes a control-to-outcome framework for assessing digital governance in large-scale skill-development administration.

## 1. Introduction

Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY), launched by the Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship (MSDE) in July 2015, was designed to enable large numbers of young people to undertake industry-relevant skill training and certification. During 2015–22, three phases of the scheme were implemented. The Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG) reports that these phases had an outlay of about ₹14,450 crore and a combined target of 1.32 crore candidates; 1.10 crore candidates were certified, while ₹10,194 crore was released and ₹9,261 crore was utilised. The scheme involved both centrally managed and State-managed components and relied on digital systems for several stages of implementation (CAG 8–9).

The scale and administrative complexity of PMKVY make information governance important. Candidates move through a chain involving registration, training, assessment, certification and placement-related reporting. Training providers, training centres, assessors, Sector Skill Councils, State Skill Development Missions, the National Skill Development Corporation and the Ministry interact with information generated at different stages. CAG describes the Skill India Portal as the technology platform used for PMKVY implementation and identifies Management Information System integration as one of the audit objectives (CAG 9, 3–4). In such an environment, the existence of a digital platform is not equivalent to reliable administration. A digital record may be incomplete, duplicated, incorrectly entered, insufficiently verified, inaccessible to the institution responsible for oversight, or unavailable when evidence is later required.

The CAG performance audit provides unusually detailed evidence for examining this distinction. It identified weaknesses in electronic-data retention, identity and contact information, beneficiary eligibility verification, monitoring controls, Centre-State data integration and feedback mechanisms. The report also examined Ministry responses and subsequent developments, including measures introduced under PMKVY 4.0. This creates an opportunity to study digital governance not simply as technology adoption but as a chain of information and control functions.

 Research on government data governance emphasises the need to organise data across the lifecycle and connect data management with auditing, oversight and administrative use (Mao et al.). 

The literature reviewed for this study therefore provides useful conceptual tools but leaves room for an integrated audit-based examination of PMKVY. PMKVY-specific research and evaluation can address implementation or outcomes, while digital-government research explains data governance and interoperability. The present study connects these strands by examining the CAG evidence across the information chain from data generation to governance use.



## 2. Conceptual and Theoretical Background

### 2.1 Digital governance and information reliability

Digital governance is often discussed in terms of platforms, online services, databases and interoperability. For public administration, however, the governance value of a digital system also depends on the reliability of the information it produces. Mao et al. develop a government data-governance framework that connects data governance with the lifecycle of government information and with auditing, oversight and administrative communication. Their approach is useful for the present study because it moves attention from the mere presence of data infrastructure to the conditions under which data can support public functions.

For a large programme such as PMKVY, data reliability can be operationalised through several related properties: completeness, validity, consistency, uniqueness, traceability and retention. Completeness concerns whether necessary fields are populated. Validity concerns whether recorded information satisfies defined rules. Uniqueness concerns whether identifiers can distinguish records appropriately. Traceability concerns whether actions can be connected to responsible actors and supporting evidence. Retention concerns whether information and evidence remain available for subsequent verification and audit. These properties are interdependent. A record that exists but has missing identifiers may not be traceable; a verified event without retained evidence may not be auditable later.

### 2.2 Interoperability as administrative usability

Interoperability is equally important because PMKVY involves Central and State institutions. Otjacques, Hitzelberger, and Feltz show that e-government information-system interoperability raises issues of identification and data sharing. Margariti et al. extend the discussion by treating organisational interoperability as a multidimensional issue involving organisational and operational conditions rather than only technical exchange.

This distinction is important for analysing PMKVY. A system may technically transmit information while still failing to provide the right information to an institution that needs it for monitoring. Conversely, separate databases can create practical problems even where individual systems operate correctly. Interoperability therefore has at least three governance dimensions in this study: information exchange, authorised access and administrative usability.

### 2.3 Monitoring and feedback as information controls

Monitoring can also be understood as an information-control process. A monitoring event is useful only when evidence of the event can be generated, validated and connected to an appropriate corrective response. The analytical chain adopted here is therefore:

**event → evidence → validation → exception → correction**

This approach avoids equating the number of inspections or digital entries with effective monitoring.

Feedback follows a similar logic. A feedback mechanism generates governance value when participant or implementation experience is collected, analysed and connected to programme adjustment. The chain is:

**experience → information → analysis → course correction**

The low volume of formal feedback identified by the audit is therefore relevant not because it proves dissatisfaction, but because it limits the information available for systematic learning.

### 2.4 Data collaboration and governance use

Schmeling, al Dakruni, and Mergel's review of data collaboration in digital government highlights the importance of governance arrangements, data management, standardisation and access in data ecosystems. This supports the present study's focus on the final stage of the information chain: governance use. Data become administratively valuable when authorised institutions can access and interpret them and when decision-makers can use the resulting information for oversight and correction.

### 2.5 Analytical proposition

On the basis of these concepts, the study proposes that:

**A digitally administered skill-development programme becomes governance-reliable when data generation, verification, evidence retention, monitoring, information sharing, feedback and outcome tracking operate as a connected control chain.**

This proposition is used as an analytical lens for interpreting the CAG findings. It is not presented as a finding of the CAG.

## 3. Statement of the Problem

PMKVY increasingly relied on digital systems for registration, training, assessment, certification, monitoring and related administrative processes. The CAG performance audit, covering the first three phases during 2015–22, nevertheless identified weaknesses across several parts of this information chain. These included inadequate retention of crucial electronic evidence, weaknesses in trainer and assessor identification records, incomplete or repeated candidate identifiers, limitations in age and qualification verification, delays in assessment and certification, deficiencies in monitoring evidence, lack of effective Centre-State data integration and very limited formal feedback.

The research problem is therefore not whether PMKVY can be described through a single success or failure judgment. Rather, it is to examine what the audit findings reveal about the reliability of information used for beneficiary verification, programme monitoring, feedback and outcome tracking, and to examine whether subsequent digital-governance measures under PMKVY 4.0 and related systems correspond to these weaknesses.

The problem is especially relevant because digital transformation can produce large volumes of administrative information without necessarily producing reliable governance information. The study therefore asks whether the controls surrounding data are strong enough to turn digital records into trustworthy evidence for oversight and course correction.

## 4. Objectives

1. To examine data-quality, data-retention and IT-control weaknesses identified in the CAG audit of PMKVY.
2. To analyse beneficiary identity and eligibility-verification weaknesses and their implications for record reliability.
3. To examine monitoring, system-validation and Centre-State data-integration issues.
4. To examine relevant digital-governance measures under PMKVY 4.0 and subsequent digital-system developments.
5. To develop an evidence-based digital-governance framework for data reliability, verification, monitoring, feedback and outcome tracking in large-scale government skill-development programmes.

## 5. Methodology

The study uses a qualitative document-analysis design supported by descriptive quantitative analysis and comparative policy/document analysis. The principal empirical source is CAG Report No. 20 of 2025, *Skill Development under Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana*. The audit covers the first three PMKVY phases during 2015–22 and was conducted at MSDE, NSDC and eight selected States: Assam, Bihar, Jharkhand, Kerala, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh. The CAG also examined relevant financial and skill-quality issues involving the National Skill Development Fund and NCVET (CAG 8–9).

Official supporting documents include the PMKVY 4.0 guidelines and later MSDE/PIB material concerning digital systems and monitoring measures. These sources are used only for the later reform layer and are kept separate from the 2015–22 main audit evidence. Academic sources are used primarily for conceptualisation of data governance, interoperability and data collaboration. The main audit period and later reform period are kept analytically separate: 2015–22 is treated as the principal audit period, while subsequent evidence is used to examine the reform trajectory.

The unit of analysis is a documented governance or audit observation and the governance function or control associated with it. Individual beneficiaries are not treated as the statistical unit of a new survey or causal study. The study therefore does not estimate individual-level effects.

Seven analytical dimensions are used: (1) data reliability, (2) beneficiary verification, (3) evidence retention, (4) monitoring and control validation, (5) interoperability and information access, (6) feedback and outcome tracking, and (7) governance use.

The analysis uses an evidence matrix, spreadsheet-based calculations and denominator checks, a thematic coding matrix, a source-verification log and MLA 9 citation control. Each major audit observation is processed through the sequence: finding, exact evidence, period and scope, governance dimension, documented implication, required control, later reform and evidence maturity.

The comparative reform analysis uses a four-level evidence-maturity scale. Level A denotes design or policy specification; Level B denotes implementation evidence; Level C denotes evidence that a control is operational; and Level D denotes credible evidence of effectiveness. This distinction prevents the existence of a guideline or platform from being treated as proof that a governance problem has been solved.

The study has several limitations. It is documentary rather than based on a new beneficiary survey or administrative-data experiment. The CAG audit has a defined institutional and State scope, and its selected-State findings cannot automatically be generalised to every State. The main audit period differs from later reform evidence. The study does not make causal claims about employment or placement outcomes. Finally, CAG's eligibility comparisons have a documented qualification-pack version limitation because the version under which training was actually imparted was not recorded for PMKVY 1.0 and about 60.95 lakh certified PMKVY 2.0/3.0 candidates (CAG 34–35).

## 6. Data Analysis and Findings

### 6.1 Data reliability and evidence retention

The first governance issue concerns the reliability and traceability of electronic records. CAG found that no policy for retention of crucial electronic records had been formulated in accordance with the relevant requirements examined by audit. As a result, information concerning participant attendance, trainer and assessor identity/contact details, and geographic information for training-centre and assessment activities under PMKVY 1.0 was not retained. RPL photographic and video evidence was also not retained centrally, and in some cases the evidence available lower in the implementation hierarchy was incomplete or not linked clearly to candidate information (CAG 24–25).

The audit connected the retention problem to the ability to produce evidence and maintain a transaction trail. This is important analytically because data reliability is not only about what is entered into a system at the time of implementation. It also concerns whether the record and supporting evidence remain available when an oversight institution later needs to verify an event.

The audit also found weaknesses in candidate and stakeholder identifiers. Among PMKVY 2.0/3.0 participant records, 90,66,264 of 95,90,801 records had zero, Null, N/A or blank values in the bank-account-detail field, which CAG reported as 94.53 per cent. Audit also identified 12,122 unique bank-account numbers repeated for 52,381 participants. Separately, trainer and assessor identification/contact fields contained large numbers of null or zero values (CAG 26–27).

These observations should not be interpreted as proof of fraudulent beneficiaries or intentional manipulation. They indicate a weaker information-control environment in which electronic fields did not always provide the assurance expected from them. A repeated identifier may require investigation, but it is not itself evidence of intent.

The audit also examined contact information. It identified repeated email and mobile identifiers and approximately 87,000 mobile numbers with fewer than ten digits or apparent dummy patterns. Candidate email records also contained “Migrated data” for a large number of candidates. In a beneficiary-contact exercise, 4,330 beneficiaries were contacted; 36.51 per cent of emails failed, and only 171 candidates responded. These figures do not represent a representative satisfaction survey, but they provide evidence of limitations in the contactability of electronic beneficiary records (CAG 26–27).

The broader governance implication is that a digital record should not be treated as reliable merely because it exists in a central system. Completeness, uniqueness, validity, traceability and retention must be designed as control requirements.

### 6.2 Beneficiary identity and eligibility verification

The second finding concerns verification of whether the person registered for a job role actually met its prescribed entry conditions. CAG found that the online enrolment process did not contain a mechanism for verifying age/date of birth against job-role requirements. Analysis using the latest available QP Master File identified 6.54 lakh certified candidates in 705 job roles who had not attained the minimum entry age at the start of the batch. For example, 52,214 candidates in Group Farming Practitioner and 40,953 candidates in Self Employed Tailor were below the respective minimum ages used in the comparison (CAG 33–34).

This finding requires an important qualification. CAG noted that the version of the qualification pack or job role under which training was actually imparted was not recorded for PMKVY 1.0 and for about 60.95 lakh certified candidates in PMKVY 2.0/3.0. The audit therefore used the latest available QP Master File for comparison. The finding is consequently evidence of a gap in verification and record versioning, but individual cases should not be represented without this methodological limitation.

The problem continued into the later phase examined by CAG. In updated PMKVY 4.0 data as of 9 October 2024, the audit found 1.18 lakh underage candidates across 365 job roles and concluded that the corrective mechanism for excluding ineligible candidates had not yet been fully implemented at the time examined (CAG 34–35). This is significant for the reform analysis because it shows that the introduction of new digital controls cannot automatically be treated as proof of operational effectiveness.

Education data showed similar weaknesses. CAG's analysis found that among candidates certified for job roles requiring education above Class 9, required information was not recorded for 6,77,807 candidates, while 8,09,046 did not meet the minimum educational qualification identified in the comparison. The audit also identified substantial gaps for job roles requiring prior technical qualifications (CAG 36–37).

Work-experience information created another control issue. The system had a provision to record work experience for relevant job roles, but the mechanism for ensuring that the requirement was complied with had not been brought into effect. The governance lesson is therefore broader than a missing field: a system may contain a place to record information without having an effective control that validates the information.

The audit also found that the Skill India Portal lacked information on beneficiaries' employment status or school/college dropout history, limiting the ability to verify whether the scheme's targeted beneficiary criteria were being followed. The Ministry subsequently reported work toward UDISE+ integration. Under PMKVY 4.0, the official guidelines provide for candidate registration on Skill India Digital with e-KYC/Aadhaar-based authentication and mobile-number authentication (Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship, 4.0 Guidelines; CAG 37–38).

Together, these findings support a control model in which a beneficiary profile is only the first step. Reliable eligibility administration requires a rule, a verified input, an exception mechanism and a record of resolution.

### 6.3 Assessment, monitoring and system validation

The third area concerns the reliability and timeliness of controls after enrolment. CAG found assessment delays of up to 1,270 days after training completion. Final certification was delayed beyond seven days in 82,158 batches, involving 23.87 lakh candidates, with delays extending up to 1,257 days. These figures do not establish a causal effect on employment, but they demonstrate weaknesses in process timeliness and control continuity.

Monitoring evidence presents a related issue. The audit examined inspection records and identified cases in which the same photograph was used as evidence for different trainings, instances of the same inspector being shown as visiting multiple locations in different States on the same day, and other weaknesses in monitoring evidence. The Ministry reported subsequent measures involving individual logins and real-time geo-tagged images. The appropriate analytical question is therefore whether a monitoring signal is sufficiently validated to support an administrative decision, rather than whether an inspection record exists at all.

AEBAS provides another example. CAG found that AEBAS was not followed for RPL projects and that in 24 inspected Training Centres biometric devices were either not installed or not working. The report therefore identified incomplete implementation of a prescribed attendance control (CAG 76). This does not prove that trainees were absent; it shows that the intended attendance-verification control was not fully functioning.

The control chain can consequently be represented as:

**training event → attendance/inspection evidence → validation → exception identification → corrective action**

Weakness at any stage can reduce the assurance available to decision-makers.

### 6.4 Centre-State integration and feedback

PMKVY operates across Central and State institutional structures. CAG found that during 2015–22 there was an absence of data integration/convergence at Central and State levels. In selected States, initiatives for integration of skill-development efforts or preparation of consolidated databases were absent or still in progress. The audit concluded that this limited assurance regarding the effectiveness of coordination efforts (CAG 20–21).

The issue is not simply technical interoperability. If information generated at the national level is not available to an authorised State institution responsible for monitoring, the practical governance value of the information is reduced. Interoperability therefore has to be judged by administrative usability as well as data exchange.

The audit also found that six of the eight selected States lacked access to central-component training data from NSDC. This is particularly important because Centre-State information access is part of the governance chain. A central database can centralise information while still leaving decentralised oversight institutions with insufficient access.

Feedback presents a different but connected weakness. As of September 2022, only 73,100 trainees, less than one per cent of certified candidates, had submitted feedback across the three phases. This does not demonstrate that beneficiaries were dissatisfied or satisfied. It demonstrates that the formal feedback channel generated a limited volume of information for systematic evaluation and course correction.

Thus, the governance chain requires both information availability and information learning:

**data access → monitoring → feedback → analysis → course correction.**

### 6.5 Outcome information

CAG reported that among 56.14 lakh candidates certified under the STT/SP components across the three phases, 23.18 lakh were reported as placed, an overall rate of 41.29 per cent. The phase-level figures were 16.74 per cent for PMKVY 1.0, 51.08 per cent for PMKVY 2.0 and 13.47 per cent for PMKVY 3.0 (CAG 46).

These figures are important because they demonstrate why outcome tracking is necessary, but they do not establish why the rates differed across phases or whether digital-governance weaknesses caused the observed outcomes. CAG discussed other programme-level issues, including training alignment with skill gaps and market demand. The present study therefore uses placement data descriptively rather than as causal evidence.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Digitisation is not the same as governance reliability

The central finding is that the existence of a digital implementation platform does not guarantee reliable governance information. PMKVY generated large volumes of electronic records, but CAG identified missing fields, repeated identifiers, incomplete evidence retention, weak verification and limited information access. The problem is therefore not simply technological capacity. It is the relationship between technology and administrative control.



### 7.2 Verification is a governance control

The beneficiary findings demonstrate that digital registration is not equivalent to eligibility verification. Age, education and work-experience requirements were part of the programme's administrative rules, but the audit identified gaps between these rules and the information/control mechanisms used to enforce them.

The QP-version issue adds a second lesson. Verification requires not only the candidate's attributes but also a reliable record of the rule against which those attributes were assessed. Without versioned job-role or qualification-pack information, later audit comparison can become more difficult. Thus, version control should be considered part of administrative verification.

### 7.3 Monitoring requires a validation chain

The monitoring findings show why inspection counts or digital dashboards should not be treated as sufficient measures of control strength. A monitoring system has governance value when the evidence generated by it can be authenticated, linked to the relevant event and used to identify exceptions.



### 7.4 Interoperability must produce administrative usefulness

The Centre-State findings further demonstrate that interoperability is partly an organisational problem. The CAG's finding that selected States lacked access to central-component training data means that information availability was not uniform across the governance structure.



### 7.5 Reform should be assessed by maturity, not announcement

PMKVY 4.0 introduced or strengthened several mechanisms corresponding to earlier weaknesses, including e-KYC, candidate authentication, monitoring measures and one-year post-certification tracking. The official PMKVY 4.0 guidelines also provide for physical inspection, assessment monitoring and structured post-certification tracking through Skill India Digital. SIDH further represents a move toward a unified digital ecosystem. These developments are important, but they should not be treated as evidence that every earlier problem has been eliminated (Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship, 4.0 Guidelines).

The CAG's own examination of PMKVY 4.0 data in October 2024 found residual underage and contact-data issues. This makes an evidence-maturity approach useful. A policy specification is design evidence. Evidence that a mechanism has been deployed is implementation evidence. Evidence that it operates consistently is operational evidence. Evidence that it improves governance outcomes is effectiveness evidence. These are different claims.

The central implication is that digital governance should be evaluated through the complete information chain rather than platform existence or adoption statistics.

## 8. Conclusions

The CAG performance audit of PMKVY reveals a governance problem that extends beyond the question of whether a digital platform exists. The audit identified weaknesses in data quality, evidence retention, beneficiary verification, monitoring validation, Centre-State information access and feedback. These weaknesses occurred at different points in the administrative information chain, but they are analytically connected because each affects the reliability of information available for subsequent oversight.

The evidence also shows why later digital reform should be assessed cautiously. PMKVY 4.0 and related digital-system developments introduced or strengthened controls corresponding to several audit-identified weaknesses. However, the evidence reviewed does not establish that all such controls are fully operational or effective. The study therefore concludes that governance reliability in large-scale digital skill-development programmes depends on the connected operation of data, verification, evidence, monitoring, interoperability, feedback and outcome controls.

## 9. Policy Suggestions and the Way Forward

First, programme authorities should monitor data quality at the control level rather than treating database size as an indicator of reliability. Completeness, uniqueness, validity and timeliness indicators should be linked to responsible institutions and reviewed regularly.

Second, eligibility verification should be rule-based. Age, education, job-role requirements and relevant prior experience should be checked against structured rules before training begins, with exceptions routed for resolution.

Third, the exact qualification-pack and job-role version applicable to each training and assessment event should be recorded. This would reduce ambiguity in subsequent verification and audit.

Fourth, a formal evidence-retention register should specify what evidence is retained, who owns it, where it is stored, how long it is retained, who can access it and how the audit trail is preserved.

Fifth, monitoring should become more exception-based. Duplicate identifiers, unusual attendance patterns, delayed assessments, incomplete inspection evidence and other risk signals can be prioritised for review instead of relying only on volume-based monitoring.

Sixth, Centre-State interoperability should be judged by practical access and usability. Authorised State and district institutions responsible for monitoring should receive the information necessary for their functions through standardised and traceable interfaces.

Seventh, feedback and outcome tracking should form a closed loop. Information collected after training should feed evaluation and course correction, while post-certification tracking should move beyond a single placement event where data availability permits. The objective should be to connect training, certification, employment or self-employment, retention and longer-term outcomes.

The overall way forward is therefore not simply more digitisation. It is stronger governance around the digital information lifecycle.
